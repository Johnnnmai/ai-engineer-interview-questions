What are LLM scaling laws and how do they change decisions?

How to answer it in an AI engineer interview: what is being tested, a step-by-step framework, a sample answer and the mistakes to avoid.

What the interviewer is testing

Whether you understand scaling laws as a planning tool rather than trivia: how they relate model size, data and compute, and how inference cost shifts the choice away from the compute-optimal point.

Answer framework

  1. State the finding: loss falls smoothly, roughly as a power law, as you add parameters, training tokens and compute.
  2. Give the compute-optimal result: for a fixed training budget, grow parameters and tokens together, roughly 20 tokens per parameter (the 2022 Chinchilla paper).
  3. Say what it implied: many earlier large models were undertrained for their size.
  4. Bring in inference: a model served billions of times is cheaper to run if it is smaller, so teams train smaller models on far more tokens than compute-optimal.
  5. Mention the limit: lower loss does not map one-to-one to every downstream task, so you still evaluate on the tasks you care about.

Sample answer

Scaling laws say that pre-training loss decreases predictably, close to a power law, as you scale parameters, data and compute. That predictability is what makes them useful: you can run small experiments, fit the curve and estimate what a larger run will reach before spending the budget.

The compute-optimal result from the 2022 Chinchilla work is that for a fixed training budget you should scale parameters and training tokens together, at roughly 20 tokens per parameter. So a 10-billion-parameter model would want around 200 billion tokens. Several earlier models had many parameters but too few tokens.

In practice the decision is not only about training cost. If a model will serve a huge number of requests, every parameter costs money on every request, so it often pays to train a smaller model well past the compute-optimal token count. You spend more on training once and less on inference forever.

Likely follow-ups

Why would you deliberately train past the compute-optimal point? Because serving cost dominates over a model's life; a smaller model trained on more tokens is cheaper per request at similar quality.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting the 20-tokens-per-parameter ratio as a law of nature rather than a compute-optimal estimate under specific assumptions.
  • Ignoring inference cost when choosing model size.
  • Assuming lower pre-training loss automatically means better results on your product task.
  • Confusing parameter count with active parameters in mixture-of-experts models.
